AI in State and Local Government: Public Records Law and the Transparency Paradox

Government automation faces a distinct constraint federal and private-sector deployments do not: the same records public-records law makes disclosable are often what the system needs protected.

Flow Research · 9 min read

State and local governments carry the same records-processing burden examined in the federal-procurement piece earlier in this series — permitting, benefits administration, case management, public correspondence — without the FedRAMP and CMMC scaffolding that shapes federal deployment decisions. What they carry instead is a distinct, in some ways more immediate constraint: public records law, built on a transparency principle that predates AI by generations, and that creates a genuine tension with the confidentiality practices an AI deployment might otherwise adopt by default.

1. The Transparency Paradox

Every state maintains some form of public records or freedom-of-information statute, generally presuming that government records are disclosable to any requester absent a specific statutory exemption. This is, for most purposes, a healthy and important democratic principle. It creates a specific paradox for AI deployment: the very system logs, decision records, and audit trails that this series has repeatedly recommended building — precisely to satisfy the fairness, auditability, and human-oversight expectations examined across every other industry — are themselves government records, and absent a specific exemption, may be subject to disclosure to any member of the public who requests them, including a requester with adversarial intent toward the system's security.

This does not argue against building those records — the alternative, an unauditable government AI system, is considerably worse from both an accountability and a legal standpoint. It argues for building the records with public-records law in mind from the outset: understanding which categories of logged detail carry legitimate security-sensitive exemptions (system configuration detail that could be exploited if disclosed) and which categories are exactly what transparency principles are meant to expose (the reasoning behind a specific benefits denial), rather than treating all system documentation as uniformly public or uniformly protected.

2. Algorithmic Transparency Requirements, Layered on Top

Beyond general public-records obligations, a growing number of state and local governments have adopted specific algorithmic-transparency requirements — New York City's Automated Decision Systems Task Force, established under Local Law 49 of 2017, was an early and influential example of a jurisdiction formally examining how it uses algorithmic tools in government decision-making, and a number of other jurisdictions have since adopted their own disclosure and impact-assessment requirements specifically for government use of automated systems, distinct from and layered on top of the general public-records framework already in place.

Agencies deploying AI workflow automation should track these jurisdiction-specific requirements directly rather than assuming general public-records compliance is sufficient, because an algorithmic-transparency ordinance frequently imposes affirmative disclosure obligations — publishing an inventory of automated decision systems in use, for instance — that a general records-request-driven framework does not require on its own initiative.

3. The NIST AI RMF, Applied to a Government Context

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, examined in its general form earlier in this series, translates into government use with a specific inflection: the "Govern" function's ownership and accountability expectation maps naturally onto public-sector accountability structures that already exist — an agency head or program director who is answerable not merely to internal management but ultimately to the public and to elected oversight bodies. Building AI governance around this existing public accountability structure, rather than importing a private-sector governance model wholesale, tends to produce a program that fits more naturally into how the agency is already expected to operate and account for itself.

This is a useful lens for state and local agencies specifically because it reframes AI governance not as a new, separate compliance burden layered onto existing public-accountability obligations, but as an extension of an accountability structure the agency already has, applied to a new category of tool.

4. Architecture as the Practical Answer

A fully on-premises or edge deployment for case-management and benefits-administration workflows keeps sensitive constituent data — the kind that public-records law's exemptions are specifically designed to protect, such as personal financial or medical detail submitted in a benefits application — inside the agency's own controlled infrastructure, simplifying both the security posture and the exemption analysis for records requests touching that underlying constituent data. A masked secure-cloud deployment remains appropriate for genuinely public-facing, lower-sensitivity administrative workflows — general correspondence, public meeting scheduling — where speed and cost efficiency matter more than the confidentiality concerns that dominate benefits and case-management work.

The exemption analysis itself benefits from this architectural clarity: an agency that can point to a clear, documented boundary between what a system logs for internal auditability and what constituent data it processes is far better positioned to respond quickly and defensibly to a public records request than one whose AI deployment commingled sensitive constituent data with general system logs in a way that makes redaction and exemption review slower and more error-prone.

5. Public Trust as the Underlying Currency

It is worth stating directly what is ultimately at stake in getting this balance right: state and local government operates on public trust in a way that is more immediate and more locally felt than almost any private industry examined in this series — a constituent denied benefits by an opaque algorithm, or a records request stonewalled by an agency that cannot explain its own system, produces the kind of local news story that erodes trust in government generally, not merely in the specific agency involved. An architecture that is simultaneously auditable internally and thoughtfully structured for public-records compliance externally is not merely a legal risk-management exercise — it is a direct investment in the public trust the agency depends on to function at all.

Agencies that treat transparency and security as competing values to be traded off against each other tend to under-invest in both; agencies that treat them as complementary, both served by a clear, deliberate architecture, tend to build systems that hold up under scrutiny from either direction.

6. Conclusion

State and local government AI deployment carries a distinct tension the private sector and even federal deployment do not face in quite the same form: the same system documentation that satisfies auditability and fairness expectations is itself a public record, subject to disclosure absent a deliberately considered exemption analysis. An architecture that keeps constituent data in-house and clearly separated from general system logs, and that treats public-records compliance as a design input rather than an afterthought, is what allows a government agency to build AI systems that are genuinely both accountable and secure, rather than trading one against the other by default.
