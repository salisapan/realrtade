AI Automation in Title and Escrow: Wire Fraud Exposure and the Nonpublic Information Standard

Flow Research · 6 min read

Title and escrow work is high-volume and repetitive in exactly the way automation is meant to address: closing-file review, title-search summarization, and routine correspondence with buyers, sellers, and lenders. It is also work that sits directly on top of wire instructions and closing funds, which makes the deployment decision materially different from most other back-office automation.

1. The Wire Fraud Threat Model

Business-email-compromise schemes targeting real estate closings specifically exploit the correspondence layer this kind of automation touches — intercepting or spoofing wire instructions during exactly the communication window an automation tool would summarize or draft. A workflow tool that widens the number of systems with access to closing correspondence widens the attack surface unless it is deliberately designed against this specific threat, not just against generic data-loss risk.

2. ALTA Best Practices and Nonpublic Personal Information

The American Land Title Association's Best Practices Framework — the industry's own baseline, widely required by underwriters and lenders — dedicates its Pillar 3 specifically to protecting nonpublic personal information (NPI). Title agents fall within the FTC's expanded definition of "financial institution" for Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act purposes, meaning the Safeguards Rule's written information-security-program requirement applies directly to how an agency's vendors, including any AI workflow tool, handle NPI.

3. RESPA Constraints on Automated Communication

The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act restricts referral-related content in settlement communications. Automated correspondence drafting must be built with this constraint in mind from the outset — a generic AI drafting tool with no awareness of RESPA's referral restrictions can introduce compliance issues into what looks like routine correspondence.

4. Architecture as the Practical Answer

Masking account numbers, wire details, and personal identifiers before any cloud processing — or eliminating the third party from the closing-file workflow entirely through a fully local deployment — narrows the wire-fraud attack surface and the NPI exposure simultaneously, rather than requiring two separate mitigation programs bolted onto the same workflow.

5. Conclusion

In title and escrow, "confidentiality" and "fraud prevention" are not two separate concerns to be balanced against automation's efficiency gains — they are the same architectural question, answered the same way: keep sensitive fields masked or local by default, and treat the deployment model as the primary control rather than a downstream policy.
