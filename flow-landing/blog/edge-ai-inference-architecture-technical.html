Inside a Fully Local AI Deployment: The Technical Architecture Behind On-Premises Inference

What actually changes under the hood when an AI workflow runs entirely on an organization's own infrastructure — model deployment, network isolation, and the operational tradeoffs involved.

Flow Research · 15 min read

Earlier in this series, the choice between cloud and fully local AI deployment was examined primarily as a regulatory and risk-management decision — what data classification favors which model, what a threat model implies about the appropriate architecture. This piece stays underneath that layer entirely, at the level of what actually changes technically when an organization commits to running AI inference entirely on its own infrastructure, because the operational realities of that choice shape whether it is actually achievable for a given organization at all, independent of whether it is desirable in the abstract.

1. Model Deployment Without a Cloud Provider's Infrastructure

A cloud AI service abstracts away an enormous amount of infrastructure complexity — GPU provisioning, load balancing across many concurrent requests, automatic scaling as demand fluctuates through the day, redundancy across failure domains — behind a single API endpoint the customer never has to think about or maintain. A fully local deployment reintroduces all of that complexity as something the organization itself must own directly: provisioning appropriate compute hardware, typically GPU-accelerated for any model of meaningful capability, sized correctly for the organization's actual peak concurrent usage rather than the elastic, on-demand capacity a cloud provider can offer to smooth over demand spikes the organization would otherwise have to provision hardware for permanently.

This is where model size and quantization become directly relevant operational decisions rather than abstract technical details confined to a research paper. A full-precision model may require hardware far more expensive and power-hungry than an organization's IT budget or existing data center facilities can reasonably support, while a properly quantized version of the same underlying model — compressed to use lower-precision numerical representations for its internal weights, typically moving from 16-bit or 32-bit floating-point representations down to 8-bit or even 4-bit integer representations — can run meaningfully faster on more modest hardware, at a generally small and often acceptable cost to output quality, depending on the specific task and how aggressively the quantization was applied. Organizations evaluating a fully local deployment need to understand which quantization level their chosen system actually uses, and should test whether that specific tradeoff meets their accuracy requirements for their actual workflows, rather than assuming any locally deployable model is straightforwardly equivalent to its full-precision cloud counterpart.

2. What Quantization Actually Costs, and Where It Costs the Most

The research literature on model quantization — work like Dettmers and colleagues' demonstrations of large-language-model quantization down to 8-bit precision with minimal measured accuracy loss — has made this technique considerably more practical over the last several years than it was even a short time earlier, but "minimal accuracy loss" is a claim that needs to be verified against the specific task an organization actually cares about, not accepted as a universal, task-independent guarantee. Quantization tends to degrade a model's performance unevenly rather than uniformly: straightforward summarization or classification tasks often survive aggressive quantization with barely measurable quality loss, while tasks requiring precise numerical reasoning, nuanced multi-step logical inference, or careful handling of rare edge cases can degrade more noticeably at the same quantization level, because the specific weights most important for those harder capabilities are exactly the ones most sensitive to precision loss.

The practical implication is that an organization should benchmark a quantized local model against its own real workflows and its own real documents specifically, not against a generic industry benchmark the vendor cites in its marketing material, because the gap between "performs well on standard benchmarks" and "performs well on this organization's specific, idiosyncratic document types and edge cases" is exactly where quantization's uneven degradation tends to hide until it surfaces as a real, production error affecting a real case.

3. Network Isolation and the Air-Gap Question

"Fully local" exists on a spectrum, not as a single binary state, and treating it as binary is one of the more common conceptual errors organizations make when evaluating this deployment model. At one end, a local deployment might still require periodic outbound connectivity for software updates, license validation, or telemetry — a meaningfully more contained architecture than a cloud deployment, but not fully isolated from the outside network in the strictest sense. At the other end, a genuinely air-gapped deployment has no network path to the outside internet at all, receiving updates only through a manual, physically mediated process — a technician carrying validated update media into the facility — which is the most restrictive and most operationally demanding posture, typically reserved for the most sensitive government, defense, or research environments examined elsewhere in this series.

The appropriate point on this spectrum depends on the actual threat model, not a general, unexamined preference for "more local is always better" as a matter of principle. A fully air-gapped deployment forgoes automatic security patching entirely, meaning the organization takes on direct, ongoing responsibility for tracking newly disclosed vulnerabilities and manually applying updates on its own schedule — a real, sustained operational burden that needs to be weighed honestly against the isolation benefit it provides, rather than assumed to be a strictly superior posture in every case. Organizations should be explicit, in their own architecture documentation, about exactly where on this spectrum their deployment actually sits, rather than using "on-premises" as a single undifferentiated label that obscures a real and consequential distinction an auditor or a security review will eventually need answered precisely.

4. What Stays the Same: The Masking Layer

A common misconception is that a fully local deployment eliminates the need for a data-masking layer, on the theory that if nothing leaves the organization's network, masking serves no further purpose worth the engineering investment. This misunderstands what masking actually protects against in a more complete threat model. Even within a fully local deployment, internal access controls matter — not every employee or system component needs access to every piece of unmasked sensitive data, and a masking layer that scopes access appropriately within the local environment remains a meaningful, load-bearing control against internal threats, misconfigured access permissions, and the kind of lateral movement an attacker who has already breached the network perimeter would attempt next, once inside.

The masking layer's purpose genuinely shifts, in a fully local deployment, from primarily addressing third-party exposure — the concern that dominates a cloud deployment's threat model — to primarily addressing internal least-privilege access, a different threat with a different attacker profile, but a genuinely important one that organizations dropping masking entirely upon moving to a local deployment are giving up for no corresponding security benefit, simply because the specific threat masking was originally introduced to address happened to disappear when the third party did.

5. Latency, Throughput, and the Capacity-Planning Problem

A dimension of this decision that receives less attention than security but is equally consequential for whether a local deployment actually works in practice is capacity planning under real, variable load. A cloud provider's elastic scaling absorbs demand spikes — a Monday-morning surge as an entire organization logs in and begins working, an end-of-quarter crunch as reporting deadlines concentrate usage — by provisioning additional capacity transparently and temporarily, invisible to the customer. A fixed local deployment has no equivalent elasticity unless the organization has deliberately over-provisioned hardware for its peak load, sitting comparatively idle the rest of the time, which is itself a real cost most simple hardware-versus-subscription cost comparisons fail to account for honestly.

Organizations planning a local deployment need to model their actual usage pattern — not average load, which understates the problem, but peak concurrent load, including realistic growth projections as adoption within the organization increases over time — and provision accordingly, understanding explicitly that under-provisioning produces a directly visible, immediately felt failure mode: slow response times or outright request queuing precisely when the organization needs the system most, during its own busiest, highest-stakes periods.

6. Operational Ownership and the Total Cost Question

The honest tradeoff at the center of any fully local deployment decision is operational ownership: the organization becomes responsible for hardware maintenance, model updates, security patching, capacity planning, and the specialized expertise needed to run inference infrastructure reliably over years, not just at initial launch — capabilities a cloud AI vendor bundles into its service price, invisibly, as part of what the customer is actually paying for even though the price tag never itemizes it that way. An organization without existing infrastructure expertise, or without genuine appetite to build and retain it, will find the true cost of a fully local deployment considerably higher than a simple hardware price comparison suggests, once staffing, ongoing maintenance, and the real opportunity cost of that team's attention are included honestly and completely in the calculation, rather than left out because they are harder to put a clean number on than a hardware invoice.

This is not an argument against fully local deployment for organizations whose data sensitivity genuinely requires it — for those organizations, the additional operational cost is simply the legitimate price of the control, appropriately paid and reasonably budgeted for. It is an argument for evaluating that cost honestly and completely from the outset, rather than comparing only the visible hardware line item against a cloud subscription price and concluding, prematurely, that the local option is straightforwardly cheaper before the full operational picture has actually been priced in.

7. Hybrid Architectures in Practice

Most organizations that examine this tradeoff carefully do not end up choosing a single answer for their entire operation — they build a hybrid architecture, running the most sensitive workflows fully local while routing lower-sensitivity, higher-volume workflows through a masked cloud deployment that offers meaningfully better economics and less operational burden for that specific category of work. Building this hybrid architecture well requires a workflow orchestration layer genuinely capable of routing each individual task to the appropriate backend based on its actual, current data-sensitivity classification, rather than requiring every workflow in the organization to be hard-coded, once, to a single deployment target chosen at the project's outset and never revisited as circumstances change.

This routing capability is, in practice, as important an architectural investment as the local infrastructure itself, because an organization that has built excellent local infrastructure but has no clean, automated way to route only the genuinely appropriate workflows to it ends up either underusing the expensive local capacity it built, or overusing it for workloads that never actually needed that level of control in the first place — wasting the operational investment in either direction, through a routing gap rather than through any flaw in the underlying infrastructure decision itself.

8. Conclusion

A fully local AI deployment is not merely "the same system, run somewhere else" — it involves real, consequential technical decisions about model quantization and its uneven performance costs, a genuine spectrum of network isolation choices from lightly connected to fully air-gapped, a masking layer that continues to matter for genuinely different reasons than it does in a cloud deployment, honest capacity planning against real peak load, and a complete, unflinching accounting of the operational ownership the organization is actually taking on. Organizations that understand these specifics in detail, rather than treating "on-premises" as a single simple checkbox to tick during procurement, are far better positioned to actually realize the security benefits the deployment model genuinely promises, rather than discovering the operational gaps only after the infrastructure is already in production and the gaps have become real, felt problems.
