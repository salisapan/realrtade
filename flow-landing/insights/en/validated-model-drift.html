A validated model does not stay validated

Testing an AI process before launch is necessary. It is not sufficient. The documents, clients and rules it meets change over time, and quality can slip without anyone noticing.

Aug 31, 2026·3 min read

In short

  • Drift happens when incoming data or the meaning of a correct answer changes.
  • Monitor continuously, not once a year.
  • In an approval-based process, edits and rejections are an early warning.

Two kinds of change

Data changes: a supplier switches invoice layouts, a new form version appears, clients start writing in another language. And the target changes: a new regulation, a new internal policy, a new product. In both cases a process that was right in testing can become wrong in production.

Banking learned this first

US banking supervisors addressed it more than a decade ago. The Federal Reserve's guidance on model risk management, SR 11-7, treats ongoing monitoring as part of validation: checking that a model still performs as intended as conditions change, and acting when it does not. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes the same point for AI: measurement continues after deployment.

Signals you already have

  • How often people edit a prepared action before approving it.
  • How often they reject it, and why.
  • Which kinds of request get flagged as uncertain.

A rising edit or rejection rate on one process is a clear signal, long before an error reaches a client.

Who responds

Decide in advance who owns each process, who reviews the signals and who can pause it. When something changes, the answer may be to adjust a rule or to require approval again for steps that had been running freely.

How Flow helps

Because Flow waits for approval, every edit and rejection is recorded alongside the action, so the office can see where a process needs attention.

Sources
