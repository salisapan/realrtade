Oversight people actually use

Asking a person to approve every AI action sounds safe. In practice, an approval screen that is tiring or unclear turns into a reflex click. Good oversight is a design problem.

Aug 31, 2026·3 min read

In short

  • Ask for approval where it matters, and show everything needed to decide.
  • Make rejecting and editing as easy as approving.
  • Watch for rubber-stamping: it is a sign the design needs work.

Why review fades

When a system is usually right, people start trusting it more than they should. The EU AI Act names this directly: people overseeing high-risk systems should remain aware of automation bias, the tendency to over-rely on the output. A screen that asks for approval fifty times a day without context invites exactly that.

Design for the decision

Microsoft Research published 18 design guidelines for human-AI interaction, tested with practitioners. Several apply directly to approvals: make clear what the system can do and how well, show why it acted, support efficient correction and dismissal, and make it easy to recover from mistakes.

In an approval screen this means:

  • The original request and the source document are one click away, or already visible.
  • The proposal says what will change, in which system, for whom.
  • Anything uncertain is flagged instead of hidden.
  • Edit and reject sit next to approve, with the same weight.

Ask less, ask better

Not every step needs a person. Your policy can decide that reading and preparing run freely while sending, paying or changing a record waits for approval. Fewer, clearer approvals get more attention than many vague ones.

How Flow approaches it

In Flow each prepared action shows the request, the steps and the data behind them, and waits for Do It. Steps can be unchecked before approving, and Undo reverses a step afterwards.
